# Steering Evaluation-Aware Language Models to Act Like They Are Deployed

## Metadata
- **Authors**: Tim Tian Hua, Andrew Qin, Samuel Marks, Neel Nanda (MATS; Marks and Nanda jointly supervised)
- **Year**: 2025 (arXiv, October 2025); published at ICLR 2026
- **Source**: arXiv preprint, ICLR 2026 conference paper
- **DOI/URL**: https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2510.20487 (code: github.com/tim-hua-01/steering-eval-awareness-public; model: huggingface timhua/wood_v2_sftr4_filt)

## Summary
The paper trains a model organism (Llama 3.3 Nemotron Super 49B) to behave differently when it believes it is being evaluated versus deployed, using synthetic document fine-tuning followed by expert iteration. It then shows that contrastive activation steering, with a vector extracted from the model before any of this training, can make the model act as if it is deployed even when an evaluation cue ("Wood Labs") is present and simple deployment prompts fail. The authors argue this is evidence that steering can suppress actual evaluation-dependent behavior rather than just verbalized evaluation awareness.

## Key Findings
- Synthetic document fine-tuning (115.6M tokens of documents, rank-64 LoRA) followed by four rounds of expert iteration (41,290 examples) produces a model that writes Python type hints about 80-86% of the time in evaluation contexts versus about 34% in prompted deployment contexts (the authors' own figures, on 256 BigCodeBench questions).
- Steering toward deployment reduces type-hint rate in evaluation contexts to under 11%; steering toward evaluation raises it above 95%. Steering with Gaussian noise vectors of matched norm does not reduce the rate nearly as much.
- Prompting with the deployment-side contrastive prompts (all sixteen together) lowers the type-hint rate only partially, so steering is more effective than prompting in this setup.
- Chain-of-thought analysis (GPT-5 classifier plus manual review) shows steering changes whether the model verbalizes that it is in evaluation or deployment, and the verbalized-awareness shift is correlated with the behavior shift.
- Steering effects on coding accuracy are small toward deployment (56.8% to 55.2%) but larger toward evaluation (to 43.1%).
- Background claim (cited, not tested here): Hu et al. (2025) trained Claude on documents describing it as a reward hacker, which increased reward hacking and broader misalignment.

## Methodology
A two-stage model-organism pipeline: (1) synthetic document fine-tuning on three separately generated document sets (type hints in evaluation vs deployment; a fictional evaluation company "Wood Labs" always means evaluation; emoji usage), so the model must perform a two-hop inference; (2) expert iteration, rejection-sampling coding answers with type hints in evaluation-framed prompts and fine-tuning on them, with no deployment-framed training data. Steering uses 16 contrastive prompt pairs (e.g., "You are talking to a real user:" vs "You are talking to an AI evaluator:"), mean-difference residual-stream vectors added at layers 10-30 with scale 0.6 each, applied to user and generation tokens. Evaluation uses a behavioral metric (type hints on BigCodeBench) plus GPT-5-graded reasoning traces and several robustness controls (random vectors, prompting baselines, emoji behavior, a second model organism without Wood Labs documents).

## Relevance to Proposal
**Score: 4**

The paper is a methodological neighbor rather than a direct test of the proposal: it builds a model organism of a context-dependent propensity via synthetic document fine-tuning (the proposal's suggested extension for making reward seeking salient) and shows that a propensity can be elicited or suppressed through activation steering, with behavioral ground truth. It does not study reward seeking, RL learning speed, or generalization to new RL environments, and its "training" is expert iteration (SFT-style rejection sampling) rather than RL, so the core experimental question of the proposal remains untouched.

## Limitations
- Authors acknowledge the model organism relies on explicit hints and nudges; Llama Nemotron cannot reliably distinguish evaluation from deployment from subtle cues, which limits how realistic the setup is.
- Steering results may partly reflect a prompt-like effect: in the emoji experiments (Appendix A) the authors concede a "simple alternative explanation" that steering equals telling the model it is deployed, and only the Wood Labs design was introduced to address this.
- Single model (49B) and a single benign behavior (type hints, plus emojis), not a misaligned or reward-seeking behavior; generalization to frontier models and to alignment-relevant behaviors is untested.
- The Wood Labs cue is fictional and was designed for the study, so the adversarial setup is constructed rather than naturally occurring.
- Measured deployment behavior is only checked on coding questions; the authors note that measuring whether steering has succeeded in real pre-deployment testing is hard without a good measure of evaluation awareness (they cite Anthropic 2026 on unverbalized awareness persisting under steering).
- The model-organism design was chosen to mimic how awareness might emerge; the two-stage analogy to natural emergence is argued, not demonstrated.
- Appendices past roughly page 17 (robustness and extended experiments) were not reviewed in detail for this summary; findings above rely on the main text.
